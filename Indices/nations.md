## Overview

The nations of Yanorra are the major organized political powers of the Known World. Their borders, rivalries, trade routes, and claims were shaped heavily by [The Drift](./The_Drift.md), which disrupted ocean travel, destabilized climates, and made long-distance navigation dangerous.

Modern nations are not always defined by clean borders alone. Some are old states that survived the catastrophe, while others are post-Drift powers whose authority depends on ports, defensible terrain, navigable corridors, and control of rare safe harbors.

Because Yanorra lacks satellite mapping, GPS, and reliable global navigation, political geography is not perfectly uniform across the map. Well-settled regions are documented with greater confidence, while remote coasts, disputed frontiers, and storm-battered maritime zones may remain approximate.

## Political Geography

The Known World is divided between two major continental regions: [West Yanorra](./West_Yanorra.md) and [East Yanorra](./East_Yanorra.md). These continents are separated by the [Veloku Ocean](./Veloku_Ocean.md), whose post-Drift currents and storm systems make regular transoceanic crossing extremely difficult.

The most important known connection between East and West Yanorra is the [Riftlands](./Riftlands.md) trade corridor, linking western ports to eastern markets through a dangerous but historically maintained route.

Other major geographic forces shape national power:

- The [Brooding Sea](./Brooding_Sea.md) divides parts of West Yanorra and remains one of the most dangerous known waterways.
- The [Eastvoid Ocean](./Eastvoid_Ocean.md) and [Westvoid Ocean](./Westvoid_Ocean.md) mark the uncertain limits of modern navigation.
- Northern regions such as [The Farlands](./The_Farlands.md), [Saint Aveline](./Saint_Aveline.md), and [Noxoch Isle](./Noxoch_Isle.md) remain harsh, remote, and only marginally connected to the rest of the Known World.

## Borders and Claims

National borders in Yanorra should be understood as political claims as much as precise survey lines. In stable inland regions, borders may be well recorded. Along remote coasts, disputed islands, tidal hazard zones, and abandoned territories, borders may be approximate or contested.

Cartographers typically rely on land surveys, maritime logs, treaty records, local testimony, and historical precedent. As a result, different maps may vary in how they depict disputed or poorly surveyed regions.
