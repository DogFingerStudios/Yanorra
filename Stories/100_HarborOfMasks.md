# Harbor of Masks

I grew up close enough to Alonah that it never seemed exotic in the way distant places seemed exotic. Saint Aveline belonged to storms and old northern fear, Velu belonged to maps and arguments, and the Eastvoid belonged to the sort of men who ruined their lives staring at horizons. Alonah was different. Alonah was near enough that merchants from Dima Eta had docked there, near enough that a cousin’s cousin had once claimed to spend three profitable years selling rope on its quay, near enough that parents in S’Tstutodo used it as a threat against badly behaved children. Behave yourself, they said, or we’ll send you to Alonah.

The threat worked because nobody could say exactly what would happen if one were sent there. That was the island’s power. Other places frightened people with known dangers: reefs, fever, patrols, pirates, bad roads, bad water, the usual machinery by which Yanorra tries to reduce the number of travelers. Alonah frightened people by refusing to explain itself. It sat offshore between S’Tstutodo, Ronobetu, and Hav, close enough to be useful and strange enough to remain a wound in every local map. Ships stopped there because the harbor was safe. Sailors whispered because the rest of the island was not.

In Dima Eta, everyone had heard at least three Alonah stories. There were the old stories from before The Drift, when the island had been treated less like a port than a contagion. Captains marked it on charts as a place to avoid, not because any government forbade landing there, but because the Alonah themselves did. Boats that came too near were attacked. Survey parties vanished. Fishermen who tried to shelter there in storms were driven back into the water. One retired navigation instructor at my school claimed that in the old days, before the seas became as difficult as they are now, Alonah was known among sailors simply as The Teeth. Whether this was true I cannot say, but I have noticed that seafaring men rarely invent names that gentle.

The modern island is not so openly hostile. That, in its way, makes it stranger. Alonah-Clif, the port town where outsiders are permitted, is one of the safest harbors in that part of the world. Clif means city or town in several of the coastal languages spoken between Hav and the southern reaches of Wanorra, and the name is accurate in the plainest possible sense. It is not a capital, not a metropolis, not one of those swollen port cities that have eaten their own hillsides and gone looking for more. It is a town. A useful town. A harbor town. A place of inns, chandlers, warehouses, clinics, taverns, repair yards, and low stone streets rising from a protected crescent of water.

From the sea, Alonah-Clif almost disappoints. After a lifetime of stories, I expected something more theatrical: black walls, warning fires, hooded figures staring down from cliffs, perhaps a bell tolling without explanation in the fog. Instead I arrived on a mild gray morning aboard a mail-and-cargo vessel from Dima Eta and saw shops opening, smoke lifting from chimneys, workers hauling crates, and gulls committing their usual crimes above the docks. The harbor was bordered by cliffs on either side, high enough to hide nearly everything inland. Only at Alonah-Clif did the island seem to open its mouth.

The Alonah themselves were everywhere and nowhere. I mean that literally. They moved through the town constantly, but they revealed nothing by doing so. Every one I saw was covered from head to foot in layered robes, gloves, and a fine mesh that obscured the face completely. The mesh was dark enough that one could not see eyes through it, yet thin enough that I assumed they could see out. Their hoods were deep, their sleeves long, and their hands always gloved. At a glance, and even after prolonged staring of the sort one should not admit to in print, I could not determine age, sex, expression, mood, or even bodily shape beyond broad impressions of height and movement.

That was the first rule I learned about Alonah: never say man, never say woman, never say child, never say elder. Outsiders in Alonah-Clif do this instinctively after a while. They say one of them, or a robe, or a gray, or a red. The color of the robe appears to indicate authority, though no outsider I met could produce a reliable hierarchy. Gray and brown robes were common. Blue-gray robes seemed associated with port work, clinics, or administration, though that may only reflect where I happened to see them. Red robes were rare and carried the unmistakable atmosphere of consequence. When a red robe appeared, conversations ended, disputes dissolved, and outsiders suddenly remembered appointments elsewhere.

What surprised me first was not the silence, but the kindness. On my second morning in Alonah-Clif, a Hav trader came in low and crooked, its hull scraped by reef stone somewhere beyond the western cliffs. It was not sinking quickly, but it was sinking with commitment. Before the captain finished shouting for pumps, Alonah figures were already on the quay with ropes, braces, tarps, hand pumps, and a wooden frame that looked purpose-built for holding broken ships together long enough to make repairs possible. No one shouted orders. No one asked who would pay. No one argued over jurisdiction, a miracle so rare in port towns that I considered writing an entire chapter about it.

Within minutes the Alonah had organized both the crew and half the idle outsiders standing nearby. They pointed, gestured, placed tools in hands, and moved with a calm efficiency that made panic seem embarrassingly provincial. I watched one robed figure step into thigh-deep water without hesitation, fastening a brace beneath the damaged section while waves slapped against the hull. Another directed a line of sailors hauling canvas under the ship’s side. A third seemed to understand exactly when the pump team was tiring and replaced them before anyone asked. By evening the vessel was floating evenly. By the next morning it was in a repair slip.

The Hav captain, a broad woman named Tessa Ren whom I later interviewed over stew and an alarming local liquor, tried to pay the Alonah dock office. She was politely refused. She tried again through an outsider clerk. She was refused again. Finally she purchased three crates of Alonah-made pressure gauges, a coil of their famous black rope, and several sealed tins of preserved fish, which she told me was how one properly thanked the island. “You don’t give them charity,” she said. “You buy what they make, and you shut your mouth if they pretend not to notice.”

Alonah goods are not numerous, but they are excellent. Their rope is expensive and coveted by captains who expect to survive bad water. Their sailcloth resists rot, their preserved food lasts longer than it has any right to, and their precision instruments have a reputation that extends far beyond the island’s size. Barometers, timing pieces, current gauges, cable testers, valve fittings, signal lamps, and small rugged terminal parts all pass through Alonah-Clif in quantities too modest to make anyone rich and too refined to ignore. No one can explain how a population estimated at perhaps four thousand produces such a range of specialized goods, but everyone agrees that the number cannot be much smaller than that.

This estimate came up repeatedly, usually from outsiders who had lived in the port long enough to stop romanticizing it. The reasoning was always practical rather than demographic. Alonah had clinics, workshops, repair yards, farms beyond the walls, metalwork, textiles, medical skill, electrical skill, and enough logistical discipline to run the safest harbor in the region. A single gifted craftsman can make a beautiful thing, but no single craftsman can create a supply chain. The Alonah had a society behind the walls. The question was not whether it existed, but why nearly no one had seen it.

My first attempts to interview Alonah residents were humiliating in the quiet way only failed journalism can be humiliating. As long as I behaved like a passenger or customer, I was treated with courtesy. A robed figure returned my dropped notebook before I realized it had fallen from my coat. Another helped me locate the countinghouse where arriving travelers registered lodging. At a tea stall, an Alonah attendant placed a small dish of spiced salt beside my cup after watching me misunderstand the local custom and dip bread into the wrong bowl. In every case the assistance was immediate, precise, and wordless.

The moment I behaved like a journalist, however, the air changed. I approached one robed figure near a warehouse and asked whether I might ask a few questions about the island. The figure stopped, turned toward me, made a brief gesture with two gloved fingers touching the chest and an open palm extending outward, then walked away. I tried again with another near the lower market, phrasing the request more gently. The same gesture came back. A third ignored me entirely after I introduced myself as a writer from Dima Eta, which was somehow worse than refusal.

On the fourth attempt I apparently irritated the island. I had been told that outsiders often failed because they spoke too abruptly, so I began with thanks for the harbor’s hospitality, praise for the repair work I had witnessed, and a harmless observation about the weather. The robed figure listened without moving. Encouraged by this, I asked whether the people of Alonah felt misunderstood by the outside world. Before I finished the sentence, the figure raised one hand. A red robe appeared almost immediately from the mouth of a side street, as if summoned by a signal too subtle for me to see.

The red robe did not threaten me. That would have been easier to write about. Instead it stood between me and the person I had been questioning, made the same open-palmed gesture, and waited. I explained that I intended no disrespect. The red robe remained still. I asked whether there was someone authorized to speak with foreign journalists. The red robe repeated the gesture, slower this time, as if teaching a child not to touch a stove. I thanked it, because there was nothing else to do, and retreated to the ordinary world of taverns, shops, and people willing to ruin mysteries by talking about them.

The most useful of these was Corrin Vale, a chandlery owner from Ronobetu who had lived in Alonah-Clif for just over twenty years. Corrin’s shop sat halfway up the harbor road, close enough to the docks to smell tar and salt, far enough uphill that sailors arrived slightly winded and therefore less inclined to bargain. He was a narrow, white-haired man with careful hands, a collector’s memory, and the sour amusement of someone who had answered the same questions for two decades without being believed.

“In all that time,” Corrin said, “I have never heard one speak. Not once. Not in public, not by accident, not through a wall, not in anger, not in pain, not drunk, not sick, not laughing. I have heard outsiders say they heard something beyond the gates, songs maybe, celebrations maybe, but here in Alonah-Clif they do not speak. They gesture, they write numbers, they point, they sometimes draw, and when they want you to stop asking questions, they make that little sign you’ve already seen.”

He demonstrated the gesture with two fingers to his chest and his palm outward. In his hands it looked less elegant, though perhaps all borrowed languages do. He told me that outsiders called it the polite no, but that the phrase did not quite capture its force. It was not an invitation to negotiate. It was not embarrassment. It was not fear. It was the closing of a door by someone who owned the house.

Corrin had never seen skin. He said this plainly, without melodrama. No face, no wrist, no ankle, no hair, no eye glimpsed behind mesh in strong light. He had never seen an Alonah child, never seen an Alonah funeral, never seen anyone carried out dead, never seen a wedding procession, never seen a drunken Alonah, and never seen one eat in public. He did not know whether the robed figures in Alonah-Clif represented the whole population or a particular class selected for contact with outsiders. He did not know whether they had ordinary families beyond the walls or something entirely different. He did know, he said, that it was dangerous to confuse ignorance with permission.

This led to the story of the hood. Years earlier, a sailor from Hav, drunk and encouraged by other drunk men, decided he would do what generations of sober men had only imagined. In a dockside provision shop he leapt across a counter and grabbed at the hood of an Alonah who had been tallying goods. According to witnesses, he expected cloth to come away. Instead the Alonah folded downward in apparent pain, and the hood did not shift at all. One witness told Corrin it was like watching someone try to pull off a wig that had turned out to be scalp. Another said it was like pulling hair from the root, except the entire hood seemed wrong, too fixed and too alive.

The response was immediate. Several Alonah entered the shop, restrained the sailor, and carried him through the inner gate. There was no beating, no public trial, no display of anger. He simply disappeared into the part of the island outsiders are not permitted to enter and did not come back. His companions were placed aboard a departing vessel the same day and warned never to return. As Corrin told it, the worst part was not the disappearance, but the efficiency. “People like to imagine violence as noisy,” he said. “Here it was quiet enough to hear the bottles clinking behind the bar.”

Another story followed me for days because people kept telling it in lowered voices and changing the details without altering the center. A woman from Ronobetu, traveling with family or perhaps with a merchant party, went into labor while staying in Alonah-Clif. At first the incident resembled any medical emergency. The Alonah came quickly, transported her to a clinic, and barred gawkers from crowding the street. Then, according to the innkeeper who claimed to have seen the transfer, red robes arrived. Once it was understood that she was pregnant and actively giving birth, she was taken beyond the walls. Neither mother nor child returned to the public town.

I tried to verify the account and failed in the usual Alonah fashion. Some claimed it happened twenty years earlier, others twice that. One person said the woman was from Ronobetu, another from S’Tstutodo, and a third insisted she was Hav-born but traveling under Ronobetu papers. Her name changed with every telling. What remained consistent was the horror and fascination attached to the idea that Alonah might welcome, take, rescue, recruit, or imprison pregnant women. In Dima Eta I had grown up with childhood threats about being sent to Alonah, but there was a darker adult version too, whispered around runaway daughters and women who vanished between ports.

Nobody knows what to make of these stories. The ugliest theory is that the Alonah seek outside women to refresh a closed population. A more charitable version says that some women go voluntarily, fleeing families, debts, violence, marriages, or governments that have little interest in domestic misery unless it disturbs the peace. Alonah, in this reading, is less a predator than a door. If a woman passes through it, the outside world calls her stolen because that is easier than admitting she may have chosen not to return.

Julien Voss the journalist distrusts this explanation because it is too convenient. Julien Voss the man from Dima Eta distrusts his own distrust because he has heard too many stories told by parents who would rather blame a veiled island than themselves. Alonah has become a container for disappearance. Sailors vanish there, women vanish there, criminals vanish there, children in cautionary tales vanish there, and every absence becomes proof of whatever theory one already prefers. In most places, legends grow from events. In Alonah, events seem to be swallowed by legend before anyone can get a proper look at them.

There are stories of voluntary disappearance. Corrin knew of at least three outsiders who entered Alonah-Clif, settled for a while, and one day walked to the gate without luggage, where red robes received them. A former dock clerk from Tsutodo supposedly did this after living in town for ten years. A widow from Hav was said to have gone through after selling her late husband’s ship and giving away the proceeds. A young S’Tstutodo musician, famous in a very small circle of people who mistake obscurity for genius, allegedly played three evenings in an inn, then accepted an invitation none of his friends saw delivered. In each case, the stories end at the wall.

The island’s walls are not especially tall in Alonah-Clif, which makes them more effective somehow. They do not need to be immense because the cliffs do most of the work. Alonah’s coastline rises sharply almost everywhere beyond the harbor, enclosing the island behind stone, surf, and height. From passing ships one sees cliff faces, dark vegetation above them, and at night red firelight along the shore. On certain stretches, captains report distant glows from inland settlements, not individual lamps but the collective haze of habitation. During particular seasons, when the water is calm and the wind moves seaward, crews have heard music, drums, or voices lifted in celebration beyond the cliffs.

That detail did more to humanize the Alonah for me than any theory. It is difficult to turn people into monsters after imagining them celebrating. Sailors described the sounds differently, as sailors do. One heard singing like a harvest festival. Another heard rhythmic clapping. A third claimed there were hundreds of voices and that none sounded male or female in any way he recognized, which tells us more about expectation than acoustics. No one had seen the festivals, and no one knew what was being celebrated. Still, on quiet nights, red lights burned beyond the cliffs, and the island seemed not secretive but simply elsewhere.

The Interlink rumor was more concrete, though not necessarily clearer. In the modern Yanorran sense, the Intalink is not a magical web of light and voices but a stubborn, text-driven system of cables, terminals, message boards, archives, and institutional access points. It is practical, constrained, and far less glamorous than conspiracy theorists would prefer. This has not stopped them. Several months before my visit, a maintenance vessel inspecting a cable running from S’Tstutodo toward Hav reportedly found an unauthorized splice. The branch cable ran toward Alonah, climbed the shore, and disappeared into the interior.

I met a cable technician named Merro Saye in a tavern whose floor sloped so noticeably that spilled beer made migration attempts toward the door. Merro had not been on the inspection vessel, but he knew two men who had, which in Alonah-Clif qualifies as intimate evidence. He drew the splice for me on the back of a food bill: main trunk, pressure housing, sealed collar, branch line. The work, he said, was clean. Not improvised. Not pirate work. Not the sort of crude tapping job that fails at the first serious current shift. Whoever attached that cable possessed tools, planning, and confidence.

“What did the report say?” I asked.

Merro smiled as though I had said something charmingly childish.

“What report?”

“The maintenance report.”

“There was one. Then there wasn’t.”

“Governments got involved?”

“Maybe. Companies. Harbor authorities. Cable offices. Men with stamps. Stamps are worse than soldiers in these matters.”

That answer was more Yanorran than any grand conspiracy. Our governments are not the all-seeing machines imagined in old pre-Drift political novels. Most concern themselves with defense, borders, ports, migration, taxation when they can manage it, and keeping the wrong people out or the right people in. Commerce often arranges itself, especially at sea, where practical necessity beats ideology by lunch. The disappearance of a cable report did not require a shadow empire. It required only three offices deciding the matter was troublesome and one office deciding it was expensive.

Still, the cable mattered. Rumors of Alonah spies in Yanorra are common but unconfirmed. The cable was a thing, or at least close enough to a thing that engineers discussed it with professional irritation rather than tavern excitement. If the Alonah had access to the Intalink, whether openly or secretly, they could observe message boards, market chatter, ship schedules, weather reports, public archives, political arguments, and perhaps more restricted channels if they were clever. They might not participate at all. They might simply watch.

This, more than immortality, frightened people who thought carefully about Alonah. Immortality is a fairy tale; observation is policy. A retired Ronobetu customs investigator named Avar Told gave me the most concise version over breakfast. He believed every major port between Dima Eta and Flord-Clif had at least one Alonah-connected observer, though he admitted he could prove nothing. “They don’t need armies,” he said. “They need manifests, schedules, weather reports, debts, marriages, scandals, and prices. Give me those and I can tell you where a city will be hungry before its mayor knows.”

Avar also mentioned the Ronobetu operation, though like everyone else he had not seen the official report. The story is treated in certain circles as fact wrapped in denial. Some years ago, Ronobetu allegedly sent five elite soldiers to infiltrate the island’s interior under explicit orders not to engage. Observe only. Map what they could. Determine population, capabilities, language, and threat. Two returned. Three did not. The survivors were said to have reported pale blue skin, white eyes, auburn-red hair, ordinary men and women, no visible children, agricultural settlements, communal meals at sunset, and large common sleeping halls where many Alonah rested together.

I cannot verify any of that. I include it because every serious person I spoke with knew the story and every unserious person improved it. In tavern versions, the soldiers fought through hundreds of Alonah with knives made of bone, or discovered machines older than The Drift, or saw sleeping halls where bodies hung from ceilings like fruit bats. The more sober version is unsettling enough. The soldiers hid for three nights before being discovered at night, apparently because the Alonah saw better in darkness than expected. When discovered, the settlement reacted without visible communication, converging as if every person knew what every other person knew. Whether that suggests training, signals, terrain familiarity, or something stranger depends entirely on the listener.

By my final day in Alonah-Clif, I no longer believed the most dramatic theories, but I believed the fear behind them. The Alonah are terribly distrustful people. That was the conclusion I could not escape. They are not ignorant of the world beyond their harbor; they are informed enough to choose exactly how much of it they will allow inside. They rescue ships, treat the sick, repair hulls, sell excellent instruments, and permit outsiders to sleep in their inns. They do not seem cruel. They do not seem indifferent. Yet the boundary around the interior is absolute, and anyone who mistakes kindness for openness may discover the difference too late.

On my last night I could not sleep. This is common in travel writing and usually means the writer has eaten badly, drunk unwisely, or become sentimental. In my case all three were possible, but I blamed Alonah. The harbor was quiet except for water against stone and the occasional creak of rigging. I left my inn and walked uphill through streets lit by low lamps. A few outsiders remained awake in taverns near the quay, but the town thinned quickly as I approached the inner road. The gate stood where Alonah-Clif ended and the island began.

Two guards waited on either side. They wore dark robes and face mesh like all the others, though their stillness made them seem less like people than decisions. I stopped several paces away, close enough to see the lantern light catch on the weave covering their faces and reveal nothing behind it. The road beyond the gate did not run straight inward. It turned sharply to the right behind a rock wall, a simple design that prevented any view into the interior. Even architecture participated in refusal.

As I stepped closer, more guards appeared. Not dramatically. Not from hidden trapdoors or theatrical shadows. They came from side passages and recessed doorways with such unhurried coordination that I felt foolish for being startled. They did not surround me tightly, but they arranged themselves so that every path except retreat seemed hypothetical. Their silence had weight. It was not the silence of people with nothing to say, but of people who had already decided speaking would give away too much.

“Why won’t you talk to us?” I asked.

No one moved. No one gestured. No one tilted a head in curiosity or annoyance. I tried again, because journalists are paid to mistake repetition for courage. “What do you gain by letting us come here if you refuse to let us know you? Why repair our ships? Why treat our sick? Why sell us instruments and rope and food if you want nothing from us?”

The guards gave no answer. It occurred to me then that perhaps the answer was obvious. They did want something from us. They wanted our ships, our movement, our goods, our news, our diseases understood before they spread, our technologies examined, our intentions measured, our habits recorded. They wanted the outside world available but contained. Alonah-Clif was not a welcome mat. It was a membrane.

I approached the gate. Two guards moved in unison and placed gloved hands against my chest. They did not shove. They did not seize me. The pressure was firm, almost gentle, and completely final. If the polite no was a door closing, this was the wall explaining itself. I stood there long enough to become aware of my own heartbeat and the absurdity of the notebook in my coat pocket. Then I removed a business card from its case and slipped it into the fold of the nearest guard’s robe.

“My address is on that,” I said, which may be the stupidest sentence ever spoken at the gate of Alonah. “In case anyone decides to answer.”

The guard did not react. None of them did. I wished them good evening and walked back down toward the harbor, resisting the urge to turn around until the road bent and the gate disappeared behind the stone. At the inn I slept badly and dreamed of red lights beyond cliffs, of children threatened with islands, of cables running silently beneath black water into rooms where no outsider had ever stood.

The next morning my ship departed with the tide. Alonah-Clif looked smaller from the deck, almost ordinary again: a town of shops and inns, repair slips and warehouses, a useful harbor on a dangerous route. Several robed figures stood at the end of the dock watching us go. I could not say whether they were guards, officials, workers, or merely citizens with a morning’s curiosity. I raised my hand, partly in farewell and partly in surrender.

For a moment nothing happened. Then one of them lifted a gloved hand.

It may not have been a wave. It may have been a signal to someone behind me, or an adjustment of sleeve, or a gesture in a language I did not understand. But as Alonah receded into fog and cliff-shadow, I chose to believe it was meant for me. Travel writers are permitted a few indulgences, and that one was mine.

I left with fewer answers than I had brought. I had not heard an Alonah voice, seen an Alonah face, learned an Alonah name, or walked a single step beyond the gate. I had gathered rumors, contradictions, half-records, vanished reports, frightened jokes, and the testimony of outsiders who had spent years living beside a people they could not describe. Yet I also left with a conviction stronger than any theory I had arrived with. The Alonah were not hiding because they knew nothing of the world. They were hiding because they knew enough to distrust it.

And perhaps, having seen something of the world myself, I could not honestly call them wrong.
