# Point Golqik

Gate Thaurin had the look of a city that had learned to distrust arrivals. Every cart that came through the eastern gate was counted twice, every crate chalked and stamped, every traveler asked his business by men who already seemed to know the answer and only wanted to hear whether he would lie. I had come into Velu through that same city months before and had disliked it for the usual reasons outsiders dislike such places: the forms, the waiting, the polite suspicion, the sense that every human impulse had been reduced to a schedule. By the time I left it for Point Golqik, I understood it better. Gate Thaurin was not unfriendly. It was a fist around a narrow throat, and nearly everything that passed between Duvessa and Velu had to breathe through it.

The Horizon Chain office occupied the upper floors of a customs annex overlooking the animal yards. It was not a grand place, which disappointed me until I remembered that important things in Yanorra are often built in rooms that smell of ink, hot metal, damp wool, and men who have not gone home in two days. The main wall was covered with maps of Velu, and the maps grew emptier the farther north and west one looked. Around Byad and Gate Thaurin there were towns, roads, survey marks, rail spurs, customs roads, river crossings, and the kind of bureaucratic clutter that gives comfort to governments. Five hundred miles west and a little north, at the edge of a great interior blank, there was Point Golqik, marked by a small black dot and a line of handwritten notes that looked less like information than apology.

Chief Surveyor Maresk Toven stood in front of that map with his hands clasped behind his back and explained the expedition as if he were explaining a repair to a roof. He was a spare man in his late fifties, long-faced, narrow-eyed, with gray hair cut so close to his skull that it seemed less a style than a disciplinary measure. The Horizon Chain, he said, was only as good as its anchors. A signal from Gate Thaurin could tell a vessel or caravan where it was in relation to Gate Thaurin, and a signal from Byad could do the same for Byad, but a northern traveler needed northern certainty. Point Golqik was small, yes, and inconvenient, yes, but it sat near one of the few workable approaches to Velu’s northern coast, and if the chain reached it cleanly then maps would become better, rescue routes would become less theoretical, and the blank on the wall would shrink by the width of a human hand.

“Three weeks out,” Maresk said, tapping the planned route with a dry fingertip. “Less than two back if we have no equipment and do not stop to work. We install seven intermediate nodes and the Golqik station. We confirm timing, signal overlap, ground behavior, weather effect, and return with the logs. You will not touch anything unless asked. You will not quote my men while they are tired. You will not turn a calibration fault into a ghost story.”

“I understand,” I said, though of course I did not. Men like Maresk always believe understanding is something that can be granted by instruction, as if the world itself were a cabinet and he had just shown me where the drawers were. I had been invited because the Horizon Chain Authority wanted a public account of the northern extension, and because somebody in Gate Thaurin had decided that a writer from Dima Eta would make the project seem larger than a Veluan works report. Maresk did not like this decision, but he respected authority more than preference, which meant he accepted me in the same way he accepted bad weather. He could not stop either, so he planned around them.

There were ten of us when we left Gate Thaurin. Maresk led the expedition, and under him was Saffa Ruun, the timing engineer, a broad-shouldered man with soft hands, nervous eyelids, and the humorless patience of someone who spent his life listening to clocks disagree. Ilen Varr handled the radio, and I liked him at once because he had the anxious, overcareful manner of a man who knew that invisible things could be real. Orro Pell, the mastwright and mechanic, was built like a post driven into frozen ground, and he spoke to machines as if every bolt were a lazy apprentice. Berrit Sol, our quartermaster, had charge of food, fuel, feed, canvas, spare parts, medicine, ammunition, lamps, forms, and the moral condition of the coffee. Hesk Adrune, a Gate Thaurin security officer, came with a rifle, a sidearm, a book of permits, and a face that suggested the permits had disappointed him more often than the weapons.

The two guides were Nadiq Sen and Pela Osk, though I learned quickly that “guide” was too small a word for either of them. Nadiq was perhaps forty-five, perhaps sixty, with skin darkened by weather and eyes that never seemed to rest on what everyone else was looking at. He knew the inland route west and north toward Golqik, knew where water could be found when the maps claimed none existed, and knew which abandoned waystations were safe enough to sleep in if one did not mind rats, drafts, and old smells. Pela was younger, quick with the pack animals and quicker with his contempt, but he had the gift of noticing unease before it became danger. If a horse looked wrong, Pela saw it. If a mule did not like the ground ahead, he believed the mule before he believed any man.

Our caravan consisted of two slow mechanical haulers, six riding horses, eight pack animals, and enough crates to build a small argument with the sky. The Horizon nodes themselves did not look impressive in pieces. They were mast sections, weather housings, timing cabinets, coil assemblies, grounding rods, ceramic insulators, crank chargers, sealed batteries, spools of wire, and boxes of carefully wrapped instruments that seemed too delicate to be sent into a country where even the rocks looked hungry. Orro supervised the loading with an expression of constant betrayal, while Berrit counted and recounted tins, sacks, straps, and fuel cans. By the time Gate Thaurin fell behind us, the city’s radio masts had dwindled into black pins against the pale eastern morning, and Maresk had already called me “Writer” three times.

The first week gave me very little to write about and a great deal to remember. We traveled westward through inhabited Velu at first, though inhabited is a generous word in that country. There were farms close to Gate Thaurin, then sheep stations, then fenced wind pumps, then isolated houses with roofs pinned down by stone and cable. The roads worsened in stages, as though civilization had not ended but had simply grown tired of repeating itself. At each stopping place Maresk made observations, Saffa checked timing drift against the master clock sealed in its shock case, Ilen raised Gate Thaurin on the radio, and Orro cursed whatever part of the first hauler had decided to imitate death.

The early nodes went up on high ground wherever the survey allowed. One was bolted to the foundation stones of an old customs lookout that had not looked out for anything in a hundred years. Another stood above a dry river cut where the wind came through with a sound like cloth tearing. The third was raised on a gravel rise so empty that I asked Nadiq why anyone had ever marked it on a map, and he told me that men from Gate Thaurin had a habit of naming places after the last thing that broke there. That rise, according to him, had three names, which meant it had broken three different expeditions in small ways. “This one will be called Orro’s Clutch,” Pela said, because the left hauler had refused to climb it until Orro spent two hours under its belly with a wrench and language I would not print in Dima Eta.

At night, the work acquired a dignity it did not have by day. The masts became thin black lines under Serya, the larger moon, while Mirelda moved where it pleased and made the shadows feel untrustworthy. Ilen’s radio would crackle inside the communications tent, and Gate Thaurin would answer with the calm indifference of distance. Saffa logged pulses, Maresk checked numbers, and Berrit poured coffee into tin cups that smelled faintly of lamp oil no matter how often they were washed. I tried to write the human version of what the officials would later reduce to a report: ten men extending certainty across five hundred miles of Velu’s interior, one mast at a time.

The first irregularity came on the ninth day, and none of us called it that then. We were at what Maresk had designated Camp Four, a low basin of thorn scrub and yellow grass, with the fourth node standing on a ridge behind us like a gallows built for a very thin man. It had been a cold day, and by evening the clouds had closed low enough to bruise the last light out of the sky. Ilen made the scheduled call to Gate Thaurin after supper, expecting the usual relay delays and repetition. Instead he sat up straighter, pressed the headphones against his ears, and looked at Maresk with the expression of a man who has heard his own name spoken in an empty house.

Maresk crossed the tent without hurrying, which was one of his habits. He believed haste gave trouble ideas. Ilen wrote something on the pad beside the radio and turned it for him to see: GATE ASKS CAMP SIX CONFIRM. Maresk read it once, then again, and then told Ilen to request repeat and authentication. Saffa, who had been oiling the latch of the timing case, stopped with the cloth in his hand. Berrit looked over from the supply ledger, and Hesk lowered the cup he had been drinking from without setting it down.

Gate Thaurin repeated the question. They had received, they said, an urgent field report from Camp Six using Maresk’s authorization phrase. The message was poor but intelligible. Lights below the ridge. One animal unaccounted for. Weather clear. Signal unreliable. Request instruction on whether to proceed by direct route. There was a pause after Ilen copied that down, not because the message was frightening in itself, but because Camp Six did not exist. Camp Six was three days ahead of us if the weather held, and the direct route to it had not yet been chosen.

“Wrong expedition,” Maresk said, though he said it for our benefit rather than his own. He ordered Ilen to ask whether any other Horizon crew was operating in northern Velu, then stood with his arms folded while the answer crawled back through static and distance. There was no other crew. There was no other northern extension. Gate Thaurin confirmed the authorization phrase and asked whether Camp Four required assistance. Maresk took the pencil from Ilen, wrote his reply in block letters, and had him send it exactly: CAMP FOUR SECURE. NO CAMP SIX ESTABLISHED. PRIOR MESSAGE MISATTRIBUTED. CONTINUE NORMAL SCHEDULE.

No one spoke much after that. Orro muttered that radio men could lose a whole city in a drawer if you let them keep the labels, and Berrit said nothing at all while he went outside to count the animals. Hesk asked Maresk who knew his authorization phrase, and Maresk gave him a look that should have ended the matter but did not. Saffa checked the time logs twice, then a third time, and Ilen sat with the headphones around his neck long after Gate Thaurin had signed off. I remember thinking that if fear had entered the camp, it had done so politely, wiping its feet before coming in.

The next two days were worse because nothing happened. The land rolled west and a little north in long, unmemorable folds, neither beautiful nor ugly, too empty to be dramatic and too varied to be dismissed. We crossed old drainage scars, flats of pale stone, strips of scrub that rattled in the wind, and stretches of ground where the horses disliked the footing though there was nothing visible to dislike. Maresk drove us hard, perhaps because work was the only argument he could make against the message. By the time we reached the place he marked as Camp Six, everyone knew without saying so that there would be a ridge.

There was a ridge. It rose north of the camp in a dark line, not high enough to be called a mountain and not low enough to ignore. Below it the land fell into a shallow bowl thick with scrub and broken stone. We arrived late, with the haulers coughing, the animals sour, and a wind moving steadily over the grass from the northwest. Maresk put the camp south of the rise, farther from the bowl than he might have otherwise, and sent Orro and Saffa up to inspect the ground for the next node while Pela picketed the animals. I saw Nadiq standing with his face turned toward the ridge, and when I asked him what he thought of it, he said it was a ridge.

That night the lights appeared below it. They were not lanterns exactly, though lanterns were the first thing any reasonable man would call them. They moved in the bowl beneath the ridge, slow and separate, some yellow, some white, one with a bluish cast that made my teeth ache to look at. Hesk raised his rifle and Maresk told him to lower it. Orro said there must be herders, and Berrit said no herder in Velu would bring animals into that bowl after dark because there was no water and no shelter. Ilen, standing beside me with his coat unbuttoned and his radio cap still on, whispered that Gate Thaurin had said lights below the ridge, and I wished very much that he had not.

Pela found the missing horse at dawn. It had pulled free sometime in the night without breaking its line, a trick no horse had ever learned for human convenience, and gone down into the bowl. We found it standing in a patch of gray grass with its head lowered, uninjured, calm, and wet with sweat though the morning was cold. There were no tracks around it except its own, and even those seemed to begin halfway down the slope as if the animal had been set there from above. Maresk ordered the camp struck early and said the report from Gate Thaurin had been coincidence combined with poor discipline, but he did not look toward the bowl when he said it.

Sera Velt met us two days later at a ruined waystation where the map offered three possible roads and the land offered five. The building had once been a relay house or tax shelter, a square stone thing with half its roof missing and a metal sign so worn that only the bolt holes remained legible. We had stopped there because Nadiq wanted to inspect the western track before committing the haulers to it. The morning was clear, bright, and unpleasantly cold, and I remember Saffa standing with a compass in one hand and a receiver in the other, frowning as if one of them had insulted the other’s mother.

Sera came from the north on a dark horse with white scars on its legs. He was a compact man, perhaps fifty, with a short beard, heavy shoulders, and the careful stillness of someone accustomed to being watched by weather. He wore a Golqik coat of layered wool and hide, patched at the elbows, and carried no visible weapon except a long knife on his belt. Nadiq saw him first but did not call out. Pela saw Nadiq see him, which amounted to the same thing, and within a few seconds every man in camp had turned toward the rider coming down the broken track.

“Point Golqik sent me,” Sera said when he was close enough, and he spoke Veluan with the clipped northern accent that makes every sentence sound like a tool being put away. He gave his name to Maresk, then produced a folded paper sealed in oilcloth. The letter was from a local administrator at Point Golqik confirming that Sera Velt had been assigned to guide the Horizon crew through the final approaches and arrange lodging upon arrival. It was official enough in the way remote documents are official: correct seal, poor paper, practical handwriting, no waste of courtesy.

Maresk read the letter twice. “We are early,” he said, not as a question, and Sera looked at him with no expression at all. According to Sera, they were late when he left Point Golqik. According to Maresk’s schedule, our progress had been better than expected, even allowing for the delay at Camp Six and Orro’s endless war with the hauler’s left drive assembly. Sera did not argue the arithmetic. He only said that Golqik had expected us two mornings before, and that when the north road gives back a day a man should accept the gift quietly.

That was when Maresk began to dislike him. I could see it happen, not in Maresk’s face, which changed very little, but in the way he folded the letter and returned it to its oilcloth with unnecessary care. He asked Sera who had sent him, what route he had taken, whether the northern track could bear the haulers, and whether any other travelers had passed south in the last ten days. Sera answered each question plainly, but there was a fraction of delay before some of his answers, as if he were translating not from one language to another but from one version of the world to another.

The true mistake came later, after noon, when Sera walked the waystation yard with Nadiq and pointed out a shallow western cut that would save us half a day. Maresk asked why it was not marked on the survey sheets. Sera shrugged and said Gate Thaurin sheets were good for reaching places already willing to be reached. Then, as if discussing rainfall, he added that the cut avoided the ridge country and that most crews were better after they stopped hearing what answered there. No one had told him about Camp Six. No one had mentioned the lights beneath the ridge, or the report from Gate Thaurin, or the horse sweating in the dawn.

Maresk waited until Sera had gone to speak with Pela about the pack animals before he called Hesk aside. They stood near the second hauler, close enough that I could hear only because I was pretending to write down the serial number on a crate. “Keep an eye on Velt,” Maresk said. Hesk asked whether he should disarm him, and Maresk said no, not yet, because suspicion is useful only until it announces itself. From then on Hesk watched Sera with the bleak satisfaction of a man finally given a human shape for his unease.

The final approach to Point Golqik was harder than I expected. In my mind, the settlement had become an ending, and endings are supposed to offer some release from the road. Instead the land rose and flattened, rose and flattened, and the air grew colder without becoming cleaner. We passed through long interior distances where the ground sounded hollow under the horses, then through bands of dark scrub bent permanently by western wind. Once, near evening, the radio carried a line of singing under Gate Thaurin’s signal, so faint that at first I thought it was someone in camp humming. Ilen heard it too, and when I asked what it was he took off the headphones and said carrier noise can do many things when a man is tired.

The song returned the next morning while no radio was powered. It came through the guy wire of the sixth node as Orro tightened the upper stays, a vibration too regular to be wind and too thin to be called music without embarrassment. Saffa told him to stop moving. Orro took both hands off the wire. The note continued for three heartbeats, changed once, then vanished. Nobody wanted to discuss it afterward, and because nobody wanted to discuss it, I wrote it down with more care than it deserved. Months later, in Barrel, when old men spoke of singing in the radios during Launching Day, I would remember that wire and the cold sound it had made under Orro’s hands.

Point Golqik appeared not as a town but as an arrangement of survivals. Low houses stood on a broad shelf of land with their backs to the interior and their faces turned toward weather I could not yet see. Smoke came from iron chimneys. Lines of laundry snapped in the wind with a violence that seemed personal. There were workshops roofed in corrugated metal, a radio shed with two masts, fenced animal yards, stacked fuel drums, a communal storehouse, and a small administrative building painted a red that the climate had nearly beaten into brown. If five hundred people lived there, they had learned the trick of making five hundred look like fewer, as though population itself were something the north might tax if it noticed.

There was no inn. Sera arranged our lodging in a municipal house used for visiting officials, inspectors, and relatives who had not been warned properly. The house belonged, in some unclear way, to the settlement rather than a family. It had six rooms, a kitchen with a black stove, floors scrubbed pale by years of sand and soap, and windows fitted with storm shutters thick enough to stop thrown stones. Maresk, Saffa, Ilen, and Hesk took rooms there, while Orro and Berrit stayed near the equipment shed and the guides slept with the animals because Pela trusted Golqik dogs less than he trusted cold ground. I was given a small room at the back with a narrow bed, a writing table, and a window facing northwest.

The Golqik node went up on a rise beyond the last houses. Everyone in town seemed to know about the work, though few came to watch for long. They were not hostile, but neither were they impressed. Children stood at a distance until an older man sent them away with two words. A woman from the administrative office brought Maresk a packet of local measurements tied with string, and Maresk received them with the respect due any figures gathered under worse conditions than his own. Sera remained nearby all morning, saying little, while Hesk remained near Sera, saying less.

For most of the day, the work went beautifully. Orro had the mast seated before noon, Berrit’s fuel cells tested clean, the grounding rods took well, and Saffa’s timing cabinet behaved with the prim dignity of a machine that knows officials are watching. When Ilen raised Gate Thaurin, the signal came back weak but readable. By late afternoon the inland chain behind us had locked in sequence: Gate Thaurin, Node One, Node Two, Node Three, the ridge station, the western cut, the Golqik rise. Saffa allowed himself the smallest smile I had ever seen on a human face. Maresk looked at the numbers, checked them again, and wrote SUCCESSFUL INITIAL LOCK in the field log.

Then the receiver found the other signal. I did not understand what had happened at first, because technical alarm often resembles technical satisfaction to outsiders. Saffa leaned closer to the cabinet, adjusted the gain, and asked Ilen whether Gate Thaurin had any auxiliary station active northwest of us. Ilen relayed the question. Gate Thaurin replied that no such station existed. Maresk came over, read the display, and told Saffa to identify the source. Saffa said he could not identify it, only characterize it, which annoyed Maresk so much that for a moment he looked almost comforted.

The signal was northwest of Point Golqik. That was the first problem. The second problem was that northwest of Point Golqik was ocean, and not the useful kind that carried ferries, trawlers, or sanctioned trade. It was open water, bad water, the kind that made maps fade into caution and old stories. No Veluan Horizon station existed there. No Duvessan relay could be there. No sanctioned ship should have been sitting in that direction transmitting clean timing pulses compatible with the chain we had just built. Yet the pulse came in strong, rhythmic, and patient, as if it had been waiting for the Golqik mast to rise and complete a sentence.

Orro was the one who made the joke. “Anqara?” he said, because men will often throw myth at fear just to hear it bounce. He said it in the way a man says ghost or mother-in-law or tax inspector, expecting laughter or profanity to restore the correct size of the room. Berrit made a sound that might have become a laugh under better circumstances. Hesk looked toward the northwest as if he could arrest the horizon, and Ilen kept one hand on the radio key without pressing it.

Saffa did not laugh. He watched the signal trace, touched the timing sheet with one finger, and said, “Maybe.” It was one of the worst words I have ever heard spoken by an educated man. Had Nadiq said it, I might have taken it for northern mischief. Had Sera said it, Maresk might have had Hesk put him on the ground. But Saffa Ruun was a timing engineer, and when a timing engineer says maybe, he does not mean that legends are true. He means that the numbers have left a door open and he is professionally obligated not to pretend otherwise.

Maresk ordered the signal logged without speculation. He ordered Saffa to run the sequence again, Ilen to request confirmation from Gate Thaurin, and Orro to inspect the mast for cross-feed or mechanical fault. He did not ask Sera for an opinion, which may have been the first admission that Sera had become part of the problem. The sun went down while the men worked, and the signal remained. It weakened twice, strengthened once, and then settled into the same clean pulse from the same impossible bearing. At full dark, Gate Thaurin replied that they had no explanation and instructed Maresk to preserve all records pending review.

I slept badly that night in the municipal house. The wind pressed at the shutters. Somewhere in the building, pipes clicked like teeth. I dreamed of a chain laid across water, each link made from a voice repeating a name it had forgotten. When I woke, I heard men moving softly in the front room and saw gray light under my door. For one embarrassed second I thought I was a child in my father’s house, listening to adults conceal bad news in low voices.

Maresk knocked once and entered before I could answer. He wore his coat and gloves, and there was frost melting in his eyebrows. He had not slept much either, though on him fatigue looked less like weakness than further reduction. “Writer,” he said, “you’re heading back with Nadiq tomorrow.”

I sat up and asked him why, though we both knew the answer would not matter unless he wanted it to. Maresk looked around the room as if confirming that I had not secretly acquired technical value during the night. He said there were safety concerns, equipment questions, and a signal anomaly that might require the crew to remain in Golqik or move south in a different configuration. He had men to manage, instruments to protect, and a problem whose shape he did not yet understand. He did not need a journalist becoming another variable.

“I came to document the extension,” I said, and immediately disliked how small the sentence sounded. Maresk nodded as if I had made a fair point in a meeting whose outcome had already been recorded. He told me I had documented the extension. The chain was installed. The Golqik lock was real. The rest, he said, was no longer public works but field uncertainty, and field uncertainty eats bystanders first because they are the only ones not holding tools.

I argued longer than I should have. Pride does that. A man may understand perfectly well that he is being spared and still resent the form of the mercy. I told him the story was clearly not finished, and he said unfinished stories were the most common kind in the north. I told him my readers would want to know what had happened, and he said my readers could develop the discipline of wanting without receiving. Finally he leaned one hand on the writing table and spoke more quietly, which frightened me more than anger would have done.

“If this turns into nothing, you’ll be angry with me,” he said. “If it turns into something, I won’t have time to save you from it. Nadiq takes you south at first light. Two horses, light kit, no argument on the route. If Velt asks where you’ve gone, Hesk will answer him.”

That last sentence hung in the room after the rest had settled. I asked whether he thought Sera was involved, and Maresk’s face closed in the old official way. He said only that Sera knew too much before being told and too little when asked. It was the kind of judgment Maresk trusted because it contained no theory. He had not decided what Sera was, only that he did not like where the man stood in relation to the facts.

Nadiq was waiting with the horses before sunrise. He had packed little: food, water, blankets, a small field stove, spare socks, a coil of rope, a rifle, and a pouch of tools whose purposes I did not ask about because I was beginning to understand that northern competence often looks like clutter until the moment it saves you. The rest of the crew were already moving around the Golqik station. Saffa had the cabinet open. Ilen was speaking into the radio shed set. Orro stood on the rise with both hands on his hips, staring up at the mast as if trying to shame it into confession. Sera was nowhere visible, and Hesk was too visible, which meant Maresk had arranged the morning carefully.

I expected some farewell from Maresk, or perhaps I wanted one, which is not the same thing. He gave me none. He stood near the equipment sled with Berrit, reading from a clipboard while Berrit checked off fuel cans. When I looked back from the edge of the settlement, Maresk raised two fingers without looking up. It was not affection, but it was acknowledgment, and from Maresk that may have been the warmer thing.

The return was faster because it had almost no purpose except escape. Nadiq did not call it that, and I would not have called it that then, but the horses knew the difference between a journey that builds and a journey that leaves. We carried no mast sections, raised no stations, waited on no haulers, and stopped only when the animals required it or the weather refused to be ignored. Instead of following the installation route exactly, Nadiq cut south earlier than I expected and gave Camp Six a wide berth. When I asked whether that saved time, he said time was not the only thing worth saving.

For twelve days we moved through northern Velu with the chain behind us and Gate Thaurin somewhere ahead, though distance felt different on the return. The installed nodes spoke to one another now. Ilen was not there to hear them, but sometimes Nadiq’s small receiver caught their pulses when we camped on high ground. They sounded ordinary, which made them worse. A clean signal in a lonely place can begin to feel less like reassurance than surveillance. On the fifth night south, I woke to a faint thread of singing and found Nadiq sitting by the ashes with the receiver in his lap and the volume turned all the way down.

He saw that I was awake and switched it off. I asked him what it was, and he said some wires remember the men who tighten them. That was the most poetic thing I ever heard Nadiq say, and because it was Nadiq I trusted it less than a superstition. The next morning he behaved as if nothing had happened. He brewed bitter tea, checked the horses, and told me my left stirrup was rubbing because I rode like a clerk carrying soup.

I tried several times to make him tell me what Maresk feared. Nadiq would answer questions about weather, water, horses, bad ground, and once, after much effort, the proper way to repair a split boot sole with heated resin. But whenever I asked about Sera, the northwest signal, or the message from Camp Six, he became practical in a way that ended conversation. “Maresk is a map man,” he said once, while we rested in the lee of a stone outcrop. “Map men do not fear empty places. They fear empty places that answer.”

Gate Thaurin looked almost beautiful when we reached it, which is how I knew the north had done something to me. The city was still gray, crowded, officious, and suspicious, but its suspicions were human-sized. The animal yards stank. The customs men shouted. A woman selling fried cakes near the permit office cheated Nadiq by a coin and was so ordinary in her dishonesty that I nearly loved her. We stabled the horses, turned in the travel papers, and walked to the Horizon Chain annex through streets that seemed impossibly full of people who believed the world was where they had left it.

The news had arrived before us by radio and rumor, though not in any form that made sense. At first I thought Maresk had found something and gone quiet under orders. Then I thought perhaps the Golqik station had failed and the crew was delayed. The clerk at the annex would not meet my eyes, and the man behind him went to fetch someone senior. Nadiq stood beside me without removing his gloves. That frightened me because indoors Nadiq always removed his gloves.

Maresk’s crew had gone missing six days earlier. Not at Point Golqik. Not north of it. Not at the Golqik station, and not in pursuit of the northwest signal, if pursuit was even the right word for something that came from open ocean. They had left Point Golqik with Sera Velt still attached as local liaison, intending to inspect the western cut, recheck two intermediate nodes, and return south along the installed chain. Their last confirmed position placed them roughly two hundred miles south of Point Golqik, in clear weather, on workable ground, with no distress call completed.

I asked what was there. It was a stupid question, or perhaps it was the only question. The clerk began to answer with map language, saying there were no registered settlements, no permanent stations, no known hazard zones beyond ordinary northern exposure, but his voice thinned as he spoke. He looked at Nadiq as if asking permission to continue. Nadiq did not give it.

“What’s there?” I asked again, this time to him. Nadiq looked past me toward the radio room, where men were moving too quickly and speaking too softly, and for a long while I thought he would refuse me the answer. Then he said, “Nothing.”

I had spent much of my life believing that nothing was usually just what people called a place before someone wrote it down. A blank on a map, a silence in an archive, a village omitted because the road was poor or the witnesses inconvenient. I had made a career of walking into other people’s nothing and returning with names, meals, arguments, weather, children, graves, jokes, songs, and all the other furniture of the real. But Nadiq did not say the word that way. He said it as if nothing were not absence but presence, not an empty space waiting to be known but a thing with edges of its own.

That night, in Gate Thaurin, I slept in a clean room above a customs kitchen and dreamed again of the chain. It ran west and north across the interior of Velu, mast to mast, pulse to pulse, each station calling to the next in patient numbers. Beyond Point Golqik, over the dark water, something answered in time. South of Point Golqik, where the maps showed nothing, Maresk Toven and his men had gone quiet.

When I woke before dawn, the city’s official radios were already speaking. They sounded orderly, disciplined, and afraid.
