# Voices From the West

The first thing I noticed about Launching Day was that Sashai had apparently decided to ignore its own history.

For the better part of a week, people had been explaining the significance of the Voyage of Remembrance to me in the serious voices people reserve for funerals, courtrooms, and national tragedies. It was a sacred tradition, they said. A civic obligation. A memorial to the Barrel Islands and to the people who had vanished from the known world after The Drift turned a once-ordinary crossing into a question no one had been able to answer for four centuries. Depending on who was speaking, the voyage was either proof of Barrel’s faithfulness, proof of Barrel’s madness, or proof that people will do almost anything if their grandparents did it first. Yet when the day itself arrived, all the solemn language disappeared beneath flags, music, shouting vendors, sailors with bright ribbons tied around their sleeves, children with painted faces, and the smell of fried fish drifting through streets that had been awake since before dawn.

Sashai sat in a part of East Yanorra where the warm season made the beaches crowded and the sea retained enough heat to convince visitors that the world, despite all evidence to the contrary, had been designed for pleasure. In the morning light it had the washed-bright look of a city accustomed to salt, glare, and ocean wind. White stone buildings climbed the hills above the harbor. Laundry snapped from balconies. Gulls circled over the rooftops with the predatory intelligence of creatures who understood festivals better than most governments. By the time I left my hotel, the main avenue leading down to the waterfront was already packed with people moving in the same direction. Some carried flowers. Some carried bottles. Some carried children on their shoulders. Shopkeepers had decorated their windows with painted ships and paper waves, and one bakery had produced pastries shaped like the Barrel Islands, which struck me as either charming or grotesque depending on how much historical significance one was willing to assign to breakfast.

The closer I came to the harbor, the less the day resembled a memorial. It resembled a carnival, or perhaps one of those political rallies where everyone pretends not to know the speeches are beside the point. Music came from three directions at once. A drummer near a fountain was competing with a violinist under an awning, and somewhere behind them a brass player was attacking his instrument with more courage than skill. Children ran between adults carrying toy ships on sticks, staging elaborate naval disasters that ended with much shouting and very little concern for pedestrian traffic. One boy collided with my leg, apologized without looking up, and continued steering his vessel through what he informed me was bad water. That, I later thought, was as good a summary of Yanorran history as any scholar was likely to provide.

The harbor itself occupied a broad crescent-shaped bay protected by high stone cliffs, and on Launching Day it seemed as though the entire nation of Barrel had attempted to fit itself along the waterfront. Families occupied balconies and rooftops. Elderly couples sat on folding chairs with the proprietary air of people who had claimed their places before sunrise and would defend them against all comers. Small boats full of spectators drifted inside the bay, their passengers waving at sailors who were much too busy to wave back. At the center of the activity sat the three expedition vessels: *Tide’s Promise*, under Captain Harven Bale; *Northern Grace*, under Captain Rell Hask; and *Windward Star*, under Captain Ollo Darne. They were not beautiful ships, not in the way paintings had made them beautiful all week in shop windows and commemorative broadsheets. They were working vessels, broad and sturdy, with peeling paint in places, patched railings, stained decks, and engines that coughed occasionally from somewhere below like old men clearing their throats.

That made them more impressive, not less. Symbols are easy. Machines are harder. A symbolic ship can sail forever in a painting, caught eternally beneath a generous wind. A real ship has to carry fuel, water, livestock, sailors, tools, spare parts, and the accumulated expectations of a city that has spent four hundred years refusing to stop asking the same question. Watching the dockworkers load the vessels, I began to understand that the official explanation for the voyage was only half true. Yes, it was a remembrance. Yes, it was a ceremony. Yes, speeches would be made and songs would be sung and people would say the proper things about the dead. But the cargo told another story, and cargo, unlike politicians, rarely lies.

Food and water came first, or at least they were the easiest to recognize. Barrels of drinking water were rolled up gangways and lowered into the holds. Crates of preserved meat, dried fruit, grain, medicine, fuel, replacement rope, sailcloth, and mechanical parts followed. Each vessel carried enough supplies for forty days, which several harbor officials repeated with the pride of men who had memorized a number and were delighted to be asked about it. The historical crossing to the Barrel Islands, back before The Drift, had taken as little as twelve days in good weather and perhaps twenty in poor conditions. The islands lay west and slightly south across the Veloku Ocean, roughly halfway between Barrel and Duberia, and the route had once been ordinary enough that people complained about delays rather than disappearance. Forty days of provisions was more than caution. It was a statement of intent.

Then came the cargo no memorial voyage should have needed. Seed grain. Vegetable seeds. Irrigation components. Water filters. Hand tools. Axes, saws, picks, shovels, spare handles, sharpening stones. Books by the crate: histories, novels, agricultural manuals, children’s readers, mechanical guides, atlases, medical references, and stacks of blank journals tied with twine. A compact printing press required eight men, a crane, and a chorus of competing instructions to move aboard *Northern Grace*. The press possessed the dense, stubborn appearance shared by safes, anchors, and certain pieces of furniture that ruin friendships on moving day. Every time the crane lifted it a few inches, someone shouted advice; every time advice was shouted, three other men disagreed. The press itself contributed nothing, which made it the wisest participant in the operation.

Livestock provided the morning’s best entertainment. Goats objected to almost everything. Sheep objected quietly but consistently. The cattle seemed offended by the entire concept of ocean travel. Chickens, meanwhile, appeared to believe Launching Day had been organized specifically for their inconvenience. One rooster escaped from its cage just before noon and made a heroic attempt at independence, sprinting down the dock with the determination of a revolutionary fleeing an unpopular government. Six sailors gave chase. None covered himself in glory. The bird dodged beneath a wagon, emerged on the opposite side, scattered a group of tourists, and briefly disappeared into a crowd before being cornered near a stack of water barrels. The spectators applauded when it was captured, though I suspected many had been rooting for the rooster. People are sentimental that way when they have nothing personally at stake.

I found Captain Harven Bale near the gangway of *Tide’s Promise*, where he was trying to supervise three conversations, two loading operations, and one goat that had apparently developed political objections to boarding. Harven had the weathered look of a man carved by salt rather than aged by years, and on that morning he appeared less like a legendary captain than an overworked warehouse manager. When I asked whether the cargo seemed excessive for a ceremonial voyage, he gave me the patient smile of a man who had been asked the same question by better-dressed fools. He told me it depended on what I thought the ceremony was for. When I said I thought it was for remembering the islands, he said it was. When I asked about the printing press, he said it was in case someone needed printing. When I asked about the seed grain, he said it was in case someone needed seed. When I asked about the goats, he glanced toward the animal presently attempting to reverse down the gangway and said the goats were in case someone had a poor sense of gratitude.

He led me to a crate marked LETTERS FOR THE ISLANDS. Bundles of envelopes were being placed inside by sailors who handled them with more care than they had shown the tools or books. Some letters were new, their paper clean and bright. Others were old enough to have yellowed at the edges. I read a few with permission. To the descendants of House Talen, if any remain. To whoever lives where our family once lived. To the people of West Haven, from those who still remember. There were no letters to grandmothers or lost brothers or anyone who could possibly still be alive. Four hundred years is long enough to kill even the most persistent sentimentality. These letters were addressed to descendants, to communities, to possibilities. They were not messages to the living so much as wagers against oblivion.

I asked Harven whether people wrote such letters every voyage, and he said they did. Some families sent the same letter again and again. Some changed it. Some added births, deaths, marriages, apologies, recipes, grudges, whatever they thought ought to cross the water if anything ever did. When I asked whether he believed the letters would be read, he looked toward the western mouth of the harbor, where beyond the cliffs the Veloku Ocean waited in blue-white glare. He said he thought people wrote them because not writing them would feel worse. That answer stayed with me. It had the uncomfortable shape of truth.

By late afternoon, the city had swollen into something almost feverish. The official speeches were delivered from a platform near the harbor office, though most people beyond the first few rows could not hear them and did not seem to mind. Flags moved in the hot breeze. Vendors shouted themselves hoarse. Children slept against their parents’ shoulders. The crews made their final checks while families crowded as close to the ships as harbor guards allowed. The mood was not simple. It was not joy, exactly, and not grief, although both were present. It was the peculiar emotional weather produced when hope and dread occupy the same room and neither is willing to leave.

The bells began before sunset. One church rang first, somewhere uphill beyond the market district. A second answered from the old quarter. Then another, and another, until Sashai seemed to be ringing from within its stones. The sound spread across the city gradually enough that people did not react all at once. Conversations thinned. Heads turned. A shopkeeper standing near me removed his cap. A woman lifted her child higher so he could see the ships. The bells rolled over the harbor and out across the water, not mournful and not triumphant, but enormous. They made the city feel larger than it was, as if every street and rooftop and hidden courtyard had joined the crowd at the waterfront.

The lines were cast off while the bells were still ringing. *Windward Star* moved first, easing away from the dock with smoke trailing from her stack and sailors crowded along the rail. *Northern Grace* followed, then *Tide’s Promise*, slower and heavier in the water. People waved. Sailors waved back. Some of the waving was cheerful, some desperate, and some had the frantic quality of people trying to make one gesture serve too many purposes: farewell, blessing, apology, proof. As the ships entered the harbor channel, fireworks burst above them in gold, red, green, and white. The explosions echoed off the cliffs, startling birds into the sky and livestock into complaint. A cow aboard *Northern Grace* bellowed so loudly that people near me began laughing through their tears.

That was the strangest part, and perhaps the most human. The city sounded victorious even though nothing had been accomplished. No island had been reached. No mystery had been solved. No message had crossed the western ocean. Three ships had merely left harbor carrying cargo, livestock, letters, and men brave or foolish enough to follow a route that history had spent four centuries warning them against. Yet Sashai cheered as if the answer had already arrived. Watching the ships move toward the western horizon beneath the smoke of fireworks and the ringing of bells, I understood that the people of Barrel were not celebrating success. They were celebrating refusal. Refusal to forget. Refusal to stop asking. Refusal to accept that silence was the same thing as an answer.

I remained at the waterfront long after the ships had passed beyond sight. Many people did. The crowd thinned slowly, reluctantly, as though leaving too quickly might look like betrayal. By the time I returned to my hotel, the bells had stopped and the smoke had drifted out over the bay, but the city still felt charged, like air after lightning. In cafés and taverns, people spoke of the voyage in bright voices. The ships had made a fine departure. The weather looked good. The new radio equipment would change everything. For the first time, Sashai would not have to wait weeks for rumors or months for absence. This year, the ships would speak back.

The Radio House sat on a bluff above the harbor, in a squat stone building that had served so many public functions over the years that nobody seemed entirely certain what it had been built for originally. Lighthouse administration, weather bureau, merchant office, coastal communications center; the place had accumulated duties the way old men accumulate keys. I arrived late the next morning and found a crowd already gathered outside. Inside, the main room smelled of coffee, dust, wet wool, hot electrical equipment, and men who had slept badly. Operators moved between consoles beneath a large wall map showing the planned west-southwestern route across the Veloku Ocean. Three brass pins marked the ships’ expected positions, connected by red thread to Sashai. At that point the map still possessed the comforting quality of competence. Everything on it agreed with everything else.

The first transmission came shortly after noon. Static filled the room, followed by a voice so thin and crackling that for a moment it seemed less like a man speaking than a memory learning how to use machinery. Then Captain Harven Bale identified himself, and the room exhaled. His report was almost aggressively ordinary: heavy water five hours after launch, nothing unusual for the outer approaches, moderate wind, minor seasickness aboard *Northern Grace*, and a damaged livestock pen aboard *Windward Star*, repaired without injury unless one counted the dignity of the sailor kicked by a sheep. All three ships remained within visual distance, loosely arranged as planned. Their noon positions, calculated by compass, chronometer, sun angle, log line, and the inherited mathematics of sailors, placed them almost exactly where the wall map said they should be.

People applauded. Not because the news was dramatic, but because it existed. For four hundred years, ships had sailed toward the Barrel Islands and vanished into silence. Now the silence had answered in Harven Bale’s tired, practical voice, reporting rough seas and livestock trouble. A woman near the back of the room began crying quietly. A reporter from the *Sashai Ledger* wrote so quickly his pencil snapped. Neris, the young operator handling the exchange, grinned with the dazed pride of a man whose machine had just become part of history. Velen, the older operator standing beside him with a chipped blue mug in one hand, did not grin. He only marked the positions on the wall map and stepped back as if admiring a small but meaningful victory.

By the second day, the Radio House had become a public theater. Families arrived with food. Reporters occupied the same corners each morning as if assigned pews. Boys sold special broadsheets outside with headlines too large for the events they described. The ships reported favorable winds and slightly better progress than expected, enough to excite people but not enough to alarm the operators. Historical accounts allowed for good days at sea. A vessel could make surprising time under the right wind and current, especially near the warm belt, where weather could change quickly and push hard before anyone had finished predicting it. The route west and slightly south remained clear on the wall map. The pins advanced. The red thread stretched. Everyone liked looking at it.

The first oddity arrived that evening, though no one yet called it that. Harven reported lights low on the western horizon after midnight, several of them, moving independently and too steadily to be lightning. Captain Rell Hask of *Northern Grace* confirmed seeing lights as well, though he placed them farther south. Captain Ollo Darne aboard *Windward Star* reported no lights at all, only a faint greenish glow beneath the water that he assumed to be some kind of plankton bloom. That phrase did useful work for perhaps ten minutes. Then the crowd outside began telling itself the ships had seen island fires. By supper, half the city had decided the expedition was making miraculous progress, although any schoolchild with a map could have pointed out that the Barrel Islands remained many days away. Hope, once lit, is not easily corrected by geography.

On the third morning I began recognizing the regulars at the Radio House. Neris always arrived with his hair combed flat and lost the battle against humidity by noon. Velen drank coffee from his chipped blue mug and treated every journalist as a necessary but regrettable species of insect. A fisherman with one cloudy eye sat beneath the map each day and said nothing to anyone. A woman whose son served aboard *Northern Grace* brought pastries for the operators and never ate any herself. These people, along with the reporters and families and curious citizens, transformed the building from an office into a waiting room for the entire city. The radio was no longer equipment. It was a mouth.

The morning reports began normally enough, then wandered into confusion. Harven stated that *Northern Grace* was visible off his port side, perhaps two miles distant. Neris made the note without comment. Several minutes later Captain Hask came through and reported that he had not seen *Tide’s Promise* since before dawn. The room shifted slightly, not in panic but in attention. Darne then reported seeing both vessels from *Windward Star*, their lanterns plain through a bank of haze. When informed that neither ship had visual contact with him, Darne sounded irritated rather than worried. He said they were looking in the wrong direction, which produced a few laughs in the room. Sailors are allowed arrogance in matters of visibility. They spend too much of their lives staring at horizons not to claim expertise.

The laughter faded when the coordinates arrived. *Tide’s Promise* and *Northern Grace* were close enough to expectation, but *Windward Star* reported a position farther west-southwest than its speed should have allowed. Not impossible, Velen said at first, only unlikely. A good current might explain it. A navigational error might explain it better. Darne insisted his officers had taken the measurements twice, then a third time after receiving the query from Sashai. Compass, sun angle, chronometer, and log agreed closely enough that he refused to amend the report. He said their position was their position, and if the chart disagreed, he recommended Sashai correct the chart. That earned another laugh, but a smaller one.

The singing came that afternoon. It entered the room beneath Hask’s voice while he was giving a routine equipment report from *Northern Grace*. At first I thought it was interference from somewhere in Sashai: a tavern singer, perhaps, or children outside. Then Neris raised one hand for silence, and the room obeyed. The sound was faint but clear enough to possess shape. Several voices, maybe more, moving together in a slow melody that reminded me of sailors’ songs I had heard in a dozen ports without matching any of them exactly. Hask stopped speaking. Static breathed through the speaker. When Neris asked whether *Northern Grace* was transmitting music, Hask took long enough to answer that everyone in the room understood he had been listening too.

Hask said it was not them. Neris checked with the other ships. Harven denied any singing aboard *Tide’s Promise*. Darne denied it from *Windward Star* with enough irritation to suggest he considered music a personal insult. The strange part was not that nobody admitted to singing. Radio interference has many causes, most of them dull. The strange part was what Hask said next. Some of his crew recognized the tune. They could not name it, could not place it, could not identify the language, but they knew it in the maddening way a person sometimes knows the next line of a song heard only in childhood. One sailor claimed his mother had hummed something like it. Another said it was a dock song from old Barrel. A third said it was not from Barrel at all.

By evening, Sashai had changed again. The excitement remained, but something had entered it, a thread of unease so fine that many people mistook it for fascination. Outside the Radio House, arguments began over whether the lights meant land, whether the singing was a signal, whether *Windward Star* had found a faster current, whether the captains were tired, whether the equipment was faulty, whether all voyages had always sounded this strange but no one had possessed the means to hear them before. That last possibility unsettled me most. Perhaps nothing new was happening. Perhaps the only new thing was that, for the first time, the known world was listening.

The fourth day began under hard white sunlight, the kind warm seas produce when the sky looks polished and shadows seem cut with knives. Sashai itself was bright and hot, but the reports from the ships did not agree on weather. *Tide’s Promise* described dense fog and a long swell from the west. *Northern Grace* reported intermittent squalls and heavy rain. *Windward Star* reported calm water and almost no wind, though in the background of Darne’s transmission we could hear something striking the hull hard enough to make the speaker jump. When Neris asked him about the noise, Darne paused, then said his instruments recorded calm conditions. He did not say the sea was calm. He said the instruments recorded it. That distinction was small enough to miss and large enough to matter.

The position reports were worse. *Windward Star* had moved too far again, not nine hundred miles yet, not the impossible leaps that would come later, but enough that Velen stopped making jokes. He marked the new location on the wall map, stepped back, and stared at it with his blue mug hanging forgotten in one hand. A reporter asked if there had been an error. Velen said there had almost certainly been an error, though his tone suggested the error might belong to the universe rather than to the captain. Harven’s position remained plausible. Hask’s position remained plausible. Darne’s did not. Together they formed a triangle too wide for ships that had supposedly remained in loose contact since departure.

Harven, still the steadiest of the three, reported the lights again near dusk. More of them this time. Not close, not landward, not signal fires in any ordinary sense. He described them with professional restraint: points of illumination observed intermittently through fog, elevation uncertain, distance uncertain, movement inconsistent with known vessels. It was the sort of language men use when they are trying very hard not to sound like fools. Hask, meanwhile, reported that several crewmen aboard *Northern Grace* had heard singing without radio equipment. One man claimed it came from the water barrels. Another said it drifted through the hull. Hask sounded embarrassed while relaying this, and the embarrassment made the report worse. Men are often willing to exaggerate danger. They are much less willing to volunteer humiliation.

Darne’s final report that day concerned the stars. He stated that cloud cover had prevented reliable observation for most of the night, but in one brief clearing the visible stars did not correspond to the expected sky for their position. Neris asked him to repeat the report. Darne did, slowly, as though speaking to a child. The stars were wrong. Not hidden, not distorted, not difficult to read. Wrong. Velen closed his eyes when he heard that. I remember this distinctly because he was not a dramatic man. If anything, he seemed constitutionally opposed to drama. Yet there he stood beneath the wall map, eyes closed, one hand resting against the table, while Captain Darne calmly explained that the sky above *Windward Star* belonged somewhere else.

No one in the Radio House panicked. That is important to understand. Panic would have made things easier. Panic gives people something to oppose. What settled over the room instead was a kind of disciplined refusal. Operators checked equipment. Reporters checked notes. Families whispered. The fisherman with the cloudy eye remained beneath the map, his hands folded on the head of his cane. Everyone behaved as if the next report would explain the previous one. That is another human habit, perhaps our most persistent. We assume meaning is delayed rather than absent.

By the time I left the Radio House that evening, the harbor below had begun to glow with lamps. Sashai looked peaceful from the bluff, warm and ordinary and safely attached to the known world. Far beyond the western horizon, three ships continued across the Veloku Ocean toward islands they could not possibly reach for at least another week, and perhaps two. They carried forty days of food and water, livestock, letters, printing presses, seed grain, and enough hope to make fools of anyone inclined toward certainty. The radio had been built to keep the voyage connected to Sashai. By the fourth night, I was beginning to wonder whether connection was always a blessing.

By the fifth morning, the Radio House had stopped feeling like a public spectacle and started feeling like a hospital waiting room. The crowd outside remained large, but it had changed character. On the first day people had laughed and speculated and eaten pastries from paper bags while waiting for updates. Now they stood in smaller groups, speaking quietly and glancing up at the windows whenever someone inside moved too quickly. The newspapers still sent reporters, but even they had become subdued. Nobody shouted questions anymore. Nobody rushed out to announce rumors. The map on the wall had become the center of the building, and people approached it the way one approaches a sickbed, hoping for improvement and expecting worse.

The first report that morning came from *Northern Grace*, and Captain Hask sounded like a man trying very hard not to sound frightened. The singing had continued through most of the night, he said, though it no longer seemed to come from the radio. Several crewmen claimed they heard it through the hull. One insisted it came from inside a water barrel and had to be restrained after attempting to smash the barrel open with a fire axe. Another reported hearing his father calling from somewhere beyond the fog, which would have been unsettling enough without the additional detail that his father had been dead for twenty-two years. Hask did not dramatize any of this. He delivered it as a proper captain delivers unpleasant information, carefully and without unnecessary adjectives, but his voice had thinned. Some men sound louder when they are afraid. Hask sounded as though fear had begun hollowing him out from the inside.

Harven Bale came through less than an hour later, and for a little while his steadiness reassured everyone. *Tide’s Promise* had seen the lights again, more numerous now and closer, though he continued refusing to name them. He would not say town, settlement, signal, shore, or island. He said illumination. He said structures possibly observed through fog shortly before dawn. He said distance uncertain and bearing unreliable. The operators copied every word. Velen stood beside the map with his blue mug in one hand and a pencil in the other, but he did not mark anything immediately. Harven’s coordinates placed *Tide’s Promise* too far west for the time elapsed, not impossibly far if one accepted perfect winds, perfect currents, and perfect luck, but far enough that the arithmetic had begun to acquire the flavor of fantasy.

Darne’s transmission from *Windward Star* arrived near midday and did nothing to improve matters. He reported that his morning observations placed him considerably south of the expected route, then amended himself by saying that south had become a less useful word than it had been several days earlier. His compass, he said, had begun wandering, not spinning wildly as in a bad adventure story, but drifting with the slow indecision of an old man in a market. The stars, when visible, did not match the ship’s calculated position. The sun’s angle suggested one answer, the chronometer another, and the dead reckoning a third. When Neris asked which reading Darne trusted, the captain gave a short laugh that carried no humor at all and said he trusted the deck beneath his feet; everything beyond that was under review.

That sentence made its way through Sashai by evening. Men repeated it in taverns. Women repeated it in markets. Children repeated it because adults had said it with such strange expressions. By then the city understood that something was wrong, although it had not yet agreed on what kind of wrongness had entered the voyage. Some blamed equipment. Some blamed exhaustion. Some blamed storms. A few blamed the radio itself, arguing that no expedition had ever gone mad before people started listening to it go mad. I heard that theory from an old fisherman outside the Radio House who told me that certain doors stayed closed for a reason. When I asked whether he believed the ships were in danger, he looked at me as though I had asked whether water was wet.

That night, all three ships reported seeing one another in arrangements that could not be reconciled. Harven saw *Northern Grace* but not *Windward Star*. Hask saw neither ship but reported lights moving parallel to him at a distance of perhaps one mile. Darne claimed both vessels were close enough that he could see men standing on their decks, and when Neris told him this was not confirmed by either captain, Darne became furious. He said they had waved at him, and that he knew what waving looked like. Nobody laughed at that. Nobody even smiled. Velen marked the reported sightings on a separate sheet rather than on the main map, perhaps because transferring them to the wall would have made the impossibility too public.

By the sixth day, families had begun staying away. Not all of them, but enough to be noticed. The mother of a sailor aboard *Northern Grace* still came with pastries for the operators, though she stopped bringing enough for everyone. The old fisherman with the cloudy eye remained in his chair beneath the map and continued saying nothing. Reporters came and went, but the wild appetite of the first few days had cooled into something more cautious. Bad news is useful to newspapers; inexplicable news is more troublesome. It resists headlines. It refuses to become a proper story.

The morning transmission from *Windward Star* was the first to mention Saint Aveline. Darne did not say he was near it, exactly. He said that if his noon measurements were to be trusted, his vessel had crossed a distance no ship could have crossed in the time available and now occupied waters corresponding roughly to the far side of Yanorra. Neris asked him to repeat the position. Darne did. Velen checked the chart, then another, then a third. The numbers placed *Windward Star* near Saint Aveline, in the same dangerous region where I had once traveled north toward Noxoch Island and seen weather behave less like weather than intention. The room took this in badly. Not loudly, not dramatically, but badly. A reporter muttered that the captain had made an error. Velen answered without looking up from the chart. All three officers had taken the measurement, he said; if it was an error, it was a disciplined one.

Harven’s report arrived in the afternoon and brought with it the first open argument inside the Radio House. He described what appeared to be a harbor. Not lights merely, not structures glimpsed through fog, but a harbor: lamps in rows, masts, a breakwater, stone or something like stone, and shapes moving along what might have been a quay. The room reacted before he finished. People began talking over one another. Someone said the islands. Someone else said impossible. Velen struck the table with his palm hard enough to silence the nearest group, and Neris leaned toward the microphone and asked Captain Bale to confirm whether he believed he had reached the Barrel Islands.

The pause that followed lasted long enough for me to hear the hum of equipment and the wind pressing against the building. Then Harven said no, and that single word did more damage than any claim of discovery could have done. If he had declared the islands reached, people might have dismissed him as delusional or celebrated him as a hero, depending on temperament. But he did not claim that. He understood the timeline as well as anyone. The crossing could not be made in six days. Even the fastest historical voyages required roughly twelve, and those had occurred before The Drift, before the route became a maze of storms and broken currents and whatever else had turned the Veloku crossing into a graveyard of expeditions. Harven knew all of that. He had supervised the loading of forty days of provisions with his own hands. He knew where the Barrel Islands were supposed to be, and he knew he could not have reached them.

When Neris asked what he was seeing, Harven said he did not know. Behind his voice, very faintly, the singing began again. It was not loud. It did not need to be. The sound threaded itself through the static and through the room, and several people turned as if expecting to find singers standing behind them. Hask’s evening report from *Northern Grace* was worse in a different way. His crew had begun answering the voices. That was how he phrased it, and the phrasing mattered. Not hearing them. Not reporting them. Answering them. Men spoke to dead parents, lost brothers, wives left safely in Sashai, children who had not been born when the ships launched. One sailor had attempted to climb over the rail because he believed a lantern had been hung for him in a doorway just beyond the fog. Another had attacked the cook, convinced the man was hiding his sister in a storage locker. Hask had confined five men and posted guards near the boats, but even as he described these measures, a voice in the background of the transmission began shouting that land was close enough to smell bread. Then the signal fractured, filled with static, and returned with Hask breathing hard into the microphone, insisting they were maintaining order. Nobody in the Radio House believed him.

The seventh day began with rain in Sashai, warm rain that came down hard enough to flatten the dust and drive most of the crowd under awnings. Inside the Radio House, the windows fogged and the air grew close. The operators had not slept much. Neris looked as though he had aged a year in a week. Velen had stopped drinking coffee and begun drinking water from a metal cup, which somehow seemed more ominous. The wall map no longer clarified anything. The red threads stretched from Sashai into a confusion of pins, corrections, alternate positions, disputed sightings, and erased marks that had left gray smudges across the paper. At some point during the night, someone had covered part of the map with a sheet. Nobody admitted doing it. Nobody removed it.

Darne transmitted first. His voice sounded oddly cheerful, which frightened me more than panic would have. He reported that the morning calculations placed *Windward Star* inland, somewhere in the mountains of Velu. He knew this was absurd, he said. He understood ships did not sail across mountains. Nevertheless, the numbers were the numbers. The stars had confirmed them before dawn, or seemed to. The compass now pointed downward when removed from its housing. A crewman looking through the sextant had claimed to see not the horizon but a room with people standing in it. Darne paused after saying this, then added that the crewman had been relieved of duty. In the background, someone laughed steadily until another voice ordered him to stop.

*Northern Grace* never gave a proper morning report. Hask came through briefly, speaking over singing so loud the operators had to lower the volume. He said they were no longer alone. Neris asked whether he meant another vessel. Hask did not answer directly. He said there were lights under the water and voices above it. He said men were hearing their names called from inside the fog. He said one of the confined sailors had begun speaking a language no one aboard recognized, though another sailor insisted he understood every word until asked to translate it. The transmission ended with Hask saying, very calmly, that whatever they heard, they must not answer. Then came static, then the sound of many people singing, close enough that for several seconds everyone in the Radio House seemed to lean back from the speaker.

Harven’s final transmission came shortly before sunset. By then the rain had stopped in Sashai, and light from the west entered the Radio House through the windows in long yellow bars. I remember that clearly because the room looked almost peaceful in those moments, despite everything. Harven sounded exhausted but still recognizably himself. He reported lights ahead. Not scattered now. Not moving independently. Arranged. Ordered. He reported a harbor entrance marked by lamps, a shoreline that should not exist, and what appeared to be buildings rising beyond the water. Neris asked again whether he believed he had reached the Barrel Islands. This time Harven answered more quickly. He said this was not the islands.

Neris asked whether he could identify the harbor, and Harven said he could not. Neris asked whether he was altering course. A longer pause followed, and static moved beneath it like breath. Then Harven said they were lighting the harbor, and he thought they were expecting them. No one spoke. The room had become so still that I could hear rainwater dripping from the roof outside. Behind Harven’s voice came the singing again, softer this time, almost welcoming. Then another sound entered the transmission, a low mechanical groan that might have been the ship, or the radio, or something neither. Neris called Harven’s name. Harven did not answer. The signal faded, returned briefly, and carried one last fragment in a voice I could not identify. Not Harven. Not Hask. Not Darne. A human voice, or close enough to one, speaking words no one in the room understood.

Then silence.

For two days the Radio House continued calling. Operators changed frequencies, inspected equipment, raised coastal stations, contacted merchant vessels, and repeated the names of the three ships until the words seemed less like names than prayers worn smooth by use. No distress signal arrived. No wreckage was reported. No emergency beacon was heard. The sea gave nothing back. By the third day of silence, most of Sashai had accepted what people accept when hope becomes too heavy to carry in public. The ships were lost. Whether they had sunk, drifted beyond range, reached the Barrel Islands, or found the impossible harbor Harven had described remained a matter of argument, but argument was all that remained.

I returned to the Radio House on the fourth silent day. The crowd outside had dwindled to a handful of stubborn relatives, two reporters, and the cloudy-eyed fisherman, who now sat on the steps rather than beneath the map. Inside, the building felt larger than before. Empty rooms often do. The wall map remained in place, though no one had updated it since the final transmission. Neris sat at his console with headphones around his neck, staring at a notebook filled with coordinates that had become progressively less useful and more disturbing. When he saw me, he nodded but did not smile.

After a while, Neris said the next boats would have Horizon Chain receivers. I asked what that meant, partly because I wanted to know and partly because people in grief sometimes need a practical question placed in front of them. He said it was a navigation system Duvessa and Velu had been building for years: synchronized radio towers along the coasts, sending timed signals that a ship could compare in order to calculate its position. Not satellites, of course. Yanorra had no such things. This was ground-based, long-range, more accurate than dead reckoning, and better than trusting stars when the sky decided to lie. He said all this without enthusiasm. That made the explanation more convincing. Hope delivered brightly can sound like salesmanship. Hope delivered by a tired radio operator in an empty building sounds like engineering.

I asked whether he thought Horizon Chain would have helped them. Neris looked toward the map, at the pins, the threads, the impossible positions, Saint Aveline, Velu’s mountains, and the harbor that could not be the Barrel Islands because the Barrel Islands were still at least a week away when Harven saw lamps along the quay. He said that if the captains were wrong, Horizon Chain might have helped. If the instruments were wrong, it might have helped. If fatigue and weather and bad readings had turned the whole thing into nonsense, it might even have saved them. When I asked what it meant if the captains were not wrong, he was quiet for a long time before answering. Then it will not help at all, he said.

Outside, Sashai had begun returning to itself. Shops opened. Carts moved along the streets. Children shouted near the harbor. Somewhere a gull screamed with the confidence of a creature unburdened by mystery. Life resumes after disaster with an efficiency that can feel almost cruel. People still looked west, of course. They would probably look west for years. But they also bought bread, argued over prices, repaired nets, swept floors, and did all the small practical things by which civilizations survive their unanswered questions.

I asked whether there would be another expedition, and Neris seemed genuinely surprised by the question. Of course there would be, he said. Not despite this, but because of it. I thought about the letters aboard *Tide’s Promise*, the books, the printing press, the seed grain, the livestock, the fireworks over the harbor, and the bells ringing across Sashai while three ships moved west-southwest into history. I thought about Harven Bale saying this was not the islands. I thought about a harbor that could not exist being lit for ships that should not have been there. Then I looked at Neris, who had spent seven days listening to the known world come apart through a speaker and still believed someone would try again.

I asked him if he thought they arrived. He did not answer immediately. Beyond the windows, the harbor glittered in late afternoon light. The sea looked ordinary, which is one of the cruelties of the sea. It almost always does. Finally, Neris said he thought they went somewhere.
